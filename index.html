Clinical Assessment & Lesion Localisation

Orbital Apex vs Cavernous Sinus Both affect CN III, IV, V1, and VI. The key distinction: cavernous sinus lesions may additionally involve the sympathetic plexus (Horner syndrome) and CN V2, and are often associated with proptosis and chemosis due to venous obstruction. Orbital apex lesions spare V2.

Examination Approach Always introduce yourself, explain the examination, and obtain consent. Examine with the patient seated comfortably facing you. Test both sides and compare throughout.
CN I

Olfactory Nerve — Smell

▶
1
Ask the patient if they have noticed any change in their sense of smell.
2
Occlude one nostril. Hold a familiar, non-irritant smell (coffee, vanilla, cloves) below the other nostril. Ask patient to identify it.
3
Repeat on the other side with a different substance.
Do not use Avoid ammonia or alcohol — these stimulate the trigeminal nerve, not the olfactory nerve, and will give a false-positive response even with CN I damage.
Abnormal Findings Anosmia: Unilateral loss → anterior cranial fossa tumour or skull base fracture. Bilateral → meningitis, bilateral frontal trauma, or toxic causes.
Hyperosmia/Hallucinations: Uncinate seizures (temporal lobe epilepsy).
Watch: Olfactory Nerve Examination
CN II

Optic Nerve — Vision

▶
1
Visual acuity: Test each eye separately with a Snellen chart (6 metres) or near vision card. Record as 6/6, 6/9 etc.
2
Colour vision: Use Ishihara plates if available; ask patient to identify numbers within dotted patterns.
3
Visual fields by confrontation: Sit 1 metre from patient, both at eye level. Cover one eye each. Move a finger (or white-headed pin) from the periphery inward in each quadrant. Ask patient to say "now" when they first see it.
4
Pupillary reactions: Shine a light into each eye. Assess direct and consensual (opposite eye) response. Test the swinging flashlight sign for relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD).
5
Fundoscopy: In a darkened room, examine the optic disc for margin clarity, colour, and cup-to-disc ratio. Note any papilloedema or optic atrophy.
Visual Field Defects — Localisation Optic nerve lesion: Monocular blindness (ipsilateral eye only).
Optic chiasma lesion: Bitemporal hemianopia (nasal fibres decussate here; pituitary tumour classic cause).
Optic tract lesion: Contralateral homonymous hemianopia.
Temporal lobe (optic radiation): Superior homonymous quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky").
Parietal lobe (optic radiation): Inferior homonymous quadrantanopia ("pie on the floor").
Occipital cortex: Homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.
Fundoscopy: Normal vs Abnormal Normal disc: yellowish-pink, oval, sharp margins, physiological cup present.
Papilloedema: Blurred disc margins, pink/hyperaemic disc, obliterated cup, venous congestion → raised ICP or malignant hypertension.
Optic atrophy: Pale disc, sharp margins → previous optic neuritis, compression, ischaemia.
Watch: Optic Nerve Examination
CN III/IV/VI

Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducent — Eye Movements

▶
1
Inspect at rest: Look for ptosis, proptosis, or resting eye deviation (strabismus/squint).
2
Pupil examination: Size (normal 2–6 mm), symmetry, shape. Direct and consensual light reflex. Accommodation reflex (ask patient to look at distance then at your finger close up).
3
Eye movements: Ask patient to follow your finger in an "H" pattern (horizontal → upper right → lower right → centre → upper left → lower left). Keep the target within 30 cm. Ask if they see double at any point.
4
Note any nystagmus, limitation of movement, or failure of conjugate gaze. Ask about diplopia — maximal diplopia is in the direction of the paralysed muscle.
CN III Palsy (Complete) Ptosis (LPS paralysis) · Eye deviated down and out (lateral rectus + superior oblique act unopposed) · Dilated fixed pupil (parasympathetic fibres run on the outside of the nerve and are compressed early by aneurysm) · Diplopia in all directions except lateral gaze.

Pupil-sparing CN III palsy: Suggests diabetic ischaemia (ischaemia affects central fibres; parasympathetic fibres on periphery spared).

CN IV Palsy: Weakness of downward gaze when the eye is adducted (descending stairs). Patient tilts head away from the affected side to compensate. Vertical diplopia.

CN VI Palsy: Medial deviation of the eye at rest (esotropia). Cannot abduct the eye. Horizontal diplopia, worse on ipsilateral gaze.
Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia (INO) Lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF). On attempted lateral gaze: ipsilateral eye abducts with nystagmus; contralateral eye fails to adduct. Convergence is preserved. Common cause: multiple sclerosis (bilateral INO is pathognomonic of MS).
Watch: Ocular Nerve Examination (CN III, IV, VI)
CN V

Trigeminal Nerve — Face Sensation & Mastication

▶
1
Sensory: Test light touch and pin-prick in all three divisions bilaterally: V1 (forehead above eye), V2 (cheek below eye), V3 (chin/jaw, not angle of jaw — that is C2/C3). Compare sides.
2
Corneal reflex (V1 afferent / VII efferent): Ask patient to look up and away. Lightly touch the cornea — not the sclera — with a wisp of cotton wool from the side. Normal response: bilateral eye closure. Absent corneal reflex suggests ophthalmic division (V1) impairment.
3
Motor: Ask patient to clench teeth (feel masseter). Ask patient to open mouth against resistance (pterygoids). In a unilateral lesion, the jaw deviates towards the weak side on opening.
4
Jaw jerk reflex: Place your finger on patient's chin with mouth slightly open; tap with a tendon hammer. Normal: absent or minimal. Brisk jaw jerk → bilateral UMN lesion above the pons (pseudobulbar palsy).
Abnormal Findings Trigeminal neuralgia (tic douloureux): Sudden, severe, lancinating unilateral facial pain, usually V2/V3, triggered by touch, chewing, or speaking. No sensory deficit on examination.
Complete CN V lesion: Ipsilateral facial sensory loss in all three divisions, absent corneal reflex, jaw deviation to the ipsilateral side.
Watch: Trigeminal Nerve Examination
CN VII

Facial Nerve — Facial Expression

▶
1
Inspection at rest: Note any facial asymmetry, ptosis, flattening of nasolabial fold, or asymmetric mouth.
2
Upper face: "Raise your eyebrows." "Screw your eyes shut tightly — don't let me open them." (Test orbicularis oculi.)
3
Lower face: "Show me your teeth." "Puff out your cheeks." "Whistle." Note any deviation or weakness.

Why is the forehead spared in UMN lesions? The upper facial nucleus receives bilateral supranuclear input (from both hemispheres), so even if one cortex is damaged, the upper face nucleus retains contralateral input. The lower face nucleus receives only contralateral input — hence it is always affected in a unilateral UMN lesion.

CN VIII

Vestibulocochlear Nerve — Hearing & Balance

▶
1
Gross hearing: Stand 60 cm from one ear; occlude the other. Whisper a number. Ask patient to repeat it. Repeat on the other side.
2
Rinne's test (512 Hz tuning fork): Place vibrating fork on mastoid (bone conduction). When patient can no longer hear it, move fork to front of external meatus (air conduction). Normally air > bone (Rinne positive). Rinne negative (bone > air) = conductive deafness.
3
Weber's test: Place vibrating fork on the vertex of the skull. Ask: "Does it sound louder in one ear?" Normal: midline (equal). Lateralises to the better ear in sensorineural loss; lateralises to the worse ear in conductive loss.
4
Vestibular function: Assess gait, Romberg test (feet together, eyes open then closed). Note any nystagmus: direction of fast phase, and whether it changes with gaze direction.
Abnormal Findings Cochlear nerve lesion: Sensorineural deafness, tinnitus.
Vestibular nerve lesion: Vertigo (false sense of rotatory movement), nystagmus, nausea, ataxia.
Acoustic neuroma (CN VIII schwannoma): Unilateral sensorineural deafness + tinnitus + vertigo. May also compress CN VII and V (cerebellar-pontine angle lesion).
Watch: Vestibulocochlear Nerve Examination
CN IX & X

Glossopharyngeal & Vagus — Swallowing & Voice

▶
1
Voice quality: Ask patient to speak. Note dysarthria (slurred, nasal, or husky voice).
2
Palate inspection: Ask patient to say "Aah." Observe soft palate. Normally it rises symmetrically in the midline. In a unilateral vagal lesion the uvula deviates to the normal side (palate drags up towards the intact side).
3
Swallowing: Ask patient to swallow a small amount of water. Note any choking, nasal regurgitation, or difficulty.
4
Gag reflex (CN IX afferent / CN X efferent): Warn the patient. Touch the posterior pharyngeal wall on each side with an orange stick. Normal response: palate elevation and pharyngeal contraction. Absent gag = IX/X lesion. Note: an absent gag reflex alone is not pathological in isolation.
Bulbar vs Pseudobulbar Palsy Bulbar palsy (LMN): Flaccid tongue, nasal voice, absent gag, jaw jerk normal/absent. Causes: motor neuron disease, Guillain-Barré, brainstem tumour.
Pseudobulbar palsy (UMN — bilateral): Spastic tongue, emotional lability, brisk jaw jerk, exaggerated gag. Causes: bilateral strokes, MS, motor neuron disease.
Watch: Glossopharyngeal & Vagus Nerve Examination
CN XI

Accessory Nerve — Neck & Shoulder

▶
1
Sternocleidomastoid (SCM): Ask patient to turn their head against resistance. The contracting SCM muscle turns the head to the opposite side. Weakness → cannot turn head to the contralateral side. Note: right SCM turns head to the left.
2
Trapezius: Ask patient to shrug both shoulders against downward resistance from your hands. Compare sides. Weakness → cannot shrug the shoulder on the affected side.
3
Inspect for wasting of the SCM or trapezius muscle belly.
Clinical Causes Neck surgery (posterior triangle), penetrating neck trauma, jugular foramen syndrome (also involves IX and X). Isolated CN XI palsy → shoulder drop, winging of scapula, and inability to abduct arm above 90°.
Watch: Accessory Nerve Examination
CN XII

Hypoglossal Nerve — Tongue

▶
1
Inspection at rest: Ask patient to open mouth. Inspect the tongue for wasting or fasciculations (fine involuntary twitching — indicates LMN lesion).
2
Protrusion: Ask patient to stick out the tongue. In a unilateral LMN lesion the tongue deviates towards the weak (affected) side — the intact genioglossus pushes the tongue across.
3
Power: Ask patient to push the tongue against the inside of each cheek while you push from the outside.
UMN vs LMN Tongue Lesions LMN lesion (ipsilateral XII palsy): Tongue deviates to the affected side, wasting, fasciculations present.
UMN lesion (contralateral cortical/internal capsule): Tongue deviates to the affected side (contralateral to the brain lesion — remember the nucleus receives crossed input), no wasting, no fasciculations. Spastic tongue.
Watch: Hypoglossal Nerve Examination
How to Use This Section Use the clinical findings below to localise the lesion. Each panel presents the classic deficit pattern for a specific nerve or anatomical site.
Complete CN III Palsy

Signs

Ptosis (drooping eyelid)
Eye deviated down and out (lateral + inferior)
Fixed, dilated pupil (mydriasis)
Diplopia in all directions except full abduction
Think: Posterior communicating artery aneurysm (dilated pupil = surgical emergency), Weber syndrome (ipsilateral CN III + contralateral hemiplegia = midbrain), cavernous sinus pathology, tentorial herniation.
Horner Syndrome

Signs (ipsilateral)

Partial ptosis (upper eyelid drops)
Miosis (small pupil)
Anhidrosis (absent facial sweating)
Enophthalmos (sunken appearance of eye)
Causes: Sympathetic pathway lesion at any level — Pancoast tumour (lung apex), carotid artery dissection, lateral medullary syndrome, syringomyelia.

Cortical Lobe Lesions — Speech & Higher Function

Frontal Lobe

Contralateral hemiparesis / monoparesis (UMN)
Broca's aphasia (expressive — cannot speak, comprehension intact)
Behavioural change, disinhibition
Contralateral forced eye deviation
Primitive reflexes (grasp, rooting) if severe

Parietal Lobe

Contralateral discriminatory sensory loss
Inferior quadrantanopia
Gerstmann syndrome: finger agnosia, dyscalculia, dysgraphia, left-right confusion
Ideomotor apraxia

Temporal Lobe

Wernicke's aphasia (receptive — fluent but incomprehensible speech, poor comprehension)
Superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky")
Memory impairment
Complex partial seizures (olfactory aura if uncus involved)

Occipital Lobe

Contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing
Visual agnosia (cannot recognise faces or objects)
Visual illusions (distorted colour, size, number)
